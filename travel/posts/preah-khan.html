Preah Khan

Feb-Mar 2026 Historical Site 2 min read 8/10 Part of: Southeast Asia Grand Tour

Overview

Preah Khan is one of the largest complexes in the Angkor region. Its name is usually translated as "Sacred Sword." APSARA dates it to 1191, when King Jayavarman VII built it on the site associated with his victory over Cham forces and dedicated it to his father.

This was more than a temple. Inscriptions describe a huge institutional population, and the complex may have functioned as an important center of Buddhist education. Popular summaries call it a city of nearly 100,000 people with more than 1,000 teachers; the exact modern interpretation of those inscription totals deserves caution, but the scale was unquestionably urban.

Exploring the Complex

The site is large enough for two or three hours and easy to get lost in. It is more ruined than Angkor Wat, yet the collapse and the trees growing through stone give it a better atmosphere. Narrow passages make a baby carrier essential; a stroller will not work.

The doors are one of the most interesting details. As you approach the central sacred space, some openings become lower, forcing visitors to bow. Guidebooks often interpret that sequence as deliberate hierarchy, with differences between the Buddhist eastern approach and directions associated with Hindu deities.

Guardian figures stand at the gates, although some are missing. The avenue repeats Angkor Thom's naga scene, with gods and demons holding the serpent.

Color changes on the stone are striking. Red areas are commonly linked to oxidation, while green and black growth comes from lichens and other organisms. Walls contain meditating figures, lions, and monster faces.

The eastern route connects toward the Jayatataka reservoir and Neak Pean. Visiting both on the same circuit makes sense.
